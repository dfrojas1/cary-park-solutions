C5 Radiculopathy
C4–C5 Disc Level

C4–C5 disc herniation or foraminal stenosis compresses the C5 root, producing lateral shoulder and upper arm pain with deltoid and biceps weakness.

C5 RootACDFForaminotomy
  • Pain: Neck → lateral shoulder → lateral upper arm
  • Weakness: Deltoid, supraspinatus, biceps
  • Reflex: ↓ Biceps (C5–C6 shared)
  • Sensory: Lateral shoulder, lateral upper arm patch
  • Shoulder abduction <90° may be limited
  • MRI: C4–C5 foraminal compression
Cervical Radiculopathy
C6 Radiculopathy
C5–C6 Disc Level · Most Common

The most common cervical radiculopathy (~25% of cases). C5–C6 disc herniation compresses the C6 root, causing lateral forearm, thumb, and index finger pain and paresthesia.

C6 RootACDFForaminotomy
  • Pain: Neck → shoulder → lateral forearm → thumb/index
  • Weakness: Biceps, brachioradialis, wrist extensors
  • Reflex: ↓ Biceps, ↓ brachioradialis
  • Sensory: Lateral forearm, thumb, index, lateral middle
  • Positive Spurling's sign
  • MRI C5–C6 foraminal compression
Cervical Radiculopathy
C7 Radiculopathy
C6–C7 Disc Level · 2nd Most Common

C6–C7 disc herniation is the second most common cervical radiculopathy. The C7 root is compressed, producing posterior arm/forearm pain and middle finger paresthesia.

C7 RootACDFForaminotomy
  • Pain: Neck → posterior shoulder → posterior forearm → middle finger
  • Weakness: Triceps, wrist flexors, finger extensors
  • Reflex: ↓ Triceps (most specific C7 reflex)
  • Sensory: Posterior arm, middle finger
  • Triceps press-down weakness on exam
  • Often presents as deep aching arm pain
Cervical Radiculopathy
C8 Radiculopathy
C7–T1 Disc Level

C7–T1 disc herniation or foraminal stenosis compresses the C8 root. Produces medial forearm and ring/small finger paresthesia with intrinsic hand muscle weakness.

C8 RootACDFForaminotomy
  • Pain: Neck → medial arm → medial forearm → ring/small fingers
  • Weakness: FDP (ring/small), intrinsic hand muscles
  • Reflex: ↓ Finger flexors (Hoffman's)
  • Sensory: Medial forearm, ring, small finger
  • Grip strength decreased; fine motor impaired
  • Differentiate from ulnar neuropathy at elbow

Rotator Cuff Tear
Supraspinatus · Most Common

Full-thickness supraspinatus tear at its insertion on the greater tuberosity. The most common rotator cuff injury, often from chronic impingement or acute trauma.

SupraspinatusArthroscopic RepairOpen Repair
  • Pain: Lateral shoulder, worse at night and overhead
  • Weakness: Shoulder abduction (supraspinatus), external rotation
  • Positive drop-arm test, empty can test
  • MRI: tendon discontinuity, retraction, fatty atrophy
  • Acute tear: often from fall on outstretched hand
  • Massive tear: involves >2 tendons

Subacromial Impingement
Neer Stages I–III

Mechanical compression of the supraspinatus tendon and subacromial bursa between the humeral head and the undersurface of the acromion, especially with overhead arm elevation.

ImpingementSubacromial InjectionAcromioplasty
  • Pain: Lateral shoulder, painful arc 60°–120° abduction
  • Positive Neer sign, Hawkins-Kennedy test
  • Stage I: edema/hemorrhage; Stage II: fibrosis; Stage III: tear
  • X-ray: acromial spur, reduced subacromial space (<7mm)
  • Conservative: PT, NSAIDs, subacromial injection
  • Surgical: arthroscopic acromioplasty ± bursectomy
Shoulder
SLAP Tear
Superior Labrum Anterior to Posterior

Tear of the superior glenoid labrum at the biceps anchor. Common in overhead athletes (pitchers, swimmers). The biceps long head anchor detaches from the superior glenoid.

Superior LabrumBiceps TenodesisSLAP Repair
  • Pain: Deep shoulder, worse with overhead and throwing
  • Positive O'Brien (active compression) test
  • Positive biceps load test in throwers
  • MRI arthrogram: superior labral tear, fluid at anchor
  • Type II most common (anchor detachment)
  • Age >35: often treated with biceps tenodesis

Bankart Lesion
Anterior Labral Tear · Instability

Detachment of the anteroinferior glenoid labrum from the glenoid rim, typically from anterior shoulder dislocation. The labrum fails to contain the humeral head, causing recurrent instability.

Anterior LabrumBankart RepairLatarjet
  • Mechanism: Anterior dislocation (fall on abducted/ER arm)
  • Associated Hill-Sachs lesion (~80% of cases)
  • Positive apprehension / relocation test
  • MRI arthrogram: anterior labral tear, fluid in tear
  • Bony Bankart: glenoid rim fracture present
  • Latarjet: for glenoid bone loss >20–25%

AC Joint Separation
Acromioclavicular Joint · Grade I–VI

Disruption of the acromioclavicular (AC) and coracoclavicular (CC) ligaments. Grades I–II: conservative. Grade III+: often surgical with CC ligament reconstruction.

Ligament TearCC ReconstructionHook Plate
  • Mechanism: Direct blow to shoulder point (fall, tackle)
  • Sign: Elevated clavicle "piano key" deformity
  • Grade I: AC sprain; Grade II: AC tear; Grade III: +CC torn
  • X-ray: weighted views show AC gap, CC distance
  • Grade III management remains debated (operative vs. conservative)
  • Grade IV–VI: all operative
Shoulder
Frozen Shoulder
Adhesive Capsulitis · 3 Phases

Progressive fibrosis and contracture of the glenohumeral joint capsule, dramatically reducing range of motion. The axillary recess is obliterated. Joint volume drops to <10 mL (normal ~28 mL).

CapsuleManipulation Under AnesthesiaCapsular Release
  • Phase 1 (Freezing): Painful, insidious 3–9 months
  • Phase 2 (Frozen): Stiff, less pain, 9–15 months
  • Phase 3 (Thawing): Gradual recovery 15–24 months
  • ER most limited; abduction also restricted
  • Associated: diabetes, thyroid disease, post-immobilization
  • MRI: thickened rotator interval, coracohumeral ligament
